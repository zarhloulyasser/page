Practices

Bigger than inquiries. Smaller than a system. Things to do repeatedly.

“The practice is the remembering.” — Vivid Void
“In meditation, practice methods. Don’t practice results.” — Sasha Chapin

A tiny personal liturgy — by ChatGPT

Not “try to feel divine.” Practice the method, not the result.

  1. Morning — 10–20 minutes. Sit before consuming information. Broad awareness, perhaps the spacious awareness you've already discovered. At the end, spend one minute remembering: I am alive; this will end; these people will end; what I do today matters. Then no need to produce any emotion.
  2. Three tiny remembrances during the day. Literally 20–60 seconds. Stop. Feel the body. Look around. Notice space. Notice that everyone around you is conscious and temporary. Don't reason about it.
  3. One ordinary sacred act. Tea, showering, walking somewhere, eating dinner. Treat that one activity as if it deserves complete attention. Repeatedly locating the transcendent in the ordinary.
  4. Evening — 10 minutes of some combination of music, art, prayer, poetry or mortality contemplation. Use aesthetics rather than pretending to be a purely intellectual contemplative.
  5. Before sleep — two questions: What did I forget today? What became important when I remembered? No essay. Thirty seconds.

That's enough structure to test the hypothesis.

Inquiries — what are we doing with these questions?

Not angling a mind-state. Changing the sampling function of the mind.

Normally: world → automatic attention → what seems important. An inquiry inserts something: world → [question] → different attention → different evidence becomes salient.

Take: if I really understood that I could die soon, what would change? I'm not primarily looking for a sentence such as “I should spend more time with family.” The question makes the mind temporarily evaluate life under mortality being highly salient. Actions receive different values. Then the interesting question is: why did the valuations change? Maybe mortality corrected a distortion. Maybe fear distorted them. Maybe both perspectives reveal different information.

So this isn't engineering moods. It's controlled perturbations of the mind's model. Death contemplation is one perturbation. Jhāna is another. Love is another. Nondual awareness is another.

Imagine a panel of observers: ordinary ambitious Yasser; Yasser vividly aware of mortality; Yasser after deep meditation; Yasser contemplating causes / no ownership; Yasser experiencing sacredness. Then ask: what changes across them — and especially, what stays true across them? That's much stronger than declaring whichever state felt most profound to be the truth.

That's also why inquiry questions aren't meant to receive conceptual answers. They are instruments for repositioning attention. See Michael Taft — How to Do Inquiry Practice. [#6: short, immediately applicable.]
