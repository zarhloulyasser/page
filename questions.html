Questions

What would have to be true for my current conception of a good life to look deeply confused?

This might be the question behind all the others. I don't want to answer it once. I want to generate hypotheses. Maybe:

  • consciousness is radically trainable;
  • the self is much less substantial than it seems;
  • close relationships matter vastly more than achievement;
  • attention is the scarce resource rather than time;
  • some form of spiritual reality exists;
  • no such metaphysical reality exists and experiences of sacredness are constructions;
  • my current ambitions are mostly mimetic;
  • my ambitions are genuinely mine and I have been underestimating their importance;
  • some capacity I've never trained would reorganize my perception of everything else.

Several of those contradict one another. That's good. I don't want another powerful experience to immediately become the new unquestioned frame. Ordinary consciousness can fool me, but extraordinary consciousness can also be interpreted too quickly.

The interesting project is learning to move between ways of seeing while becoming increasingly good at asking: what does this way of seeing reveal, what does it hide, and what survives when I look from somewhere else? That, more than preserving one sacred state forever, seems like the deeper thing I am beginning to investigate.

What capacities could I acquire that would allow me to notice things I am currently incapable of noticing?

This occurred to me from two directions at once.

The first was personal. After watching Death Note I had something like a fifth-jhāna experience: a sense of the sacred, a godly presence, life feeling short and fragile, ordinary ambitions shrinking. Then, without deciding anything, I drifted off of it. Sleep, phone, conversation, work, plans — the ordinary configuration rebuilt itself. So I started asking why we naturally fall off of such states, and what the people who have thought about this carefully — experienced meditators — say about it.

The second was watching other people. I would see even immensely successful people missing something like meditation — missing how powerful attention is — and wonder how much was lost because of that: discoveries, insights, ways of living. Which made me wonder whether they might simply be wrong about many things. And then the symmetry hit: if they can miss something that large, what am I missing right now that would make much of what I'm doing wrong, in a sense?

I think success can be misleading here. Success is something like skill + strategy + effort, given a game. Becoming extraordinarily good at a game tells you very little about whether it's the game worth playing, or whether your representation of the world even contains the important variables. A person born without color vision could become the greatest physicist alive and still have no phenomenal distinction between red and green. Intelligence alone cannot reason its way into a missing capacity to notice.

That's why this question feels more fundamental and more consequential than "what should I believe?" or "what books should I read?" Some knowledge changes what I believe. Other knowledge — meditation, mathematics, falling in love, building a company, raising children, learning another language, serious experimentation, psychotherapy, physical disciplines, living in another society — changes what I am capable of observing in the first place. I want more of the second kind.

What beliefs of mine depend on remaining in my normal state of consciousness?

This follows directly from the same experience. In the state, mortality wasn't just the proposition "I will die" — it was perceptually obvious. Life felt fragile, other people felt different, ordinary ambitions shrank, something felt sacred. Then the state passed, and the old model quietly rebuilt itself.

So now I want to ask of any important belief: does this still seem true after two hours of meditation? After fear? After social approval? Alone? When contemplating death?

I don't have to decide that one state is the privileged truth-state. The interesting discovery is that my certainty moves. If I can watch it move, I can investigate what remains invariant across states. Those invariants seem much more interesting than whatever happens to feel obvious right now.

Which truths can only be learned by doing something?

Meditation made this category much more salient to me. Before actually concentrating the mind deeply, I could read 40 books about attention and still have a poor model of its possible range.

So I want to periodically ask: is there an important domain where I'm trying to substitute conceptual understanding for an experience or skill that would generate new evidence?

This is almost an epistemological version of exploration versus exploitation. I can spend my whole life exploiting a model that would have changed dramatically after one sufficiently different experience.

If I really understood that I could die relatively soon, what would change?

Not: what dramatic thing would I do if I had one week? That's a different question.

Rather: which parts of my current allocation of attention would become obviously strange?

I think this is closer to what Death Note touched in me. Mortality doesn't necessarily imply abandoning ambition. Socrates certainly didn't conclude that. It puts pressure on the reason for the ambition.

When something goes well, what had to happen for this to happen?

I don't want to replace "I did this" with "I did nothing." That becomes false in the other direction. My effort matters. My choices matter. But normally my mind compresses hundreds of causes into "I did this." I want to expand it again: genes, parents, teachers, culture, luck, health, tools, other people's work, past experiences, present effort.

My effort is one real cause. But where did my capacity for effort come from? My intelligence? My interests? The particular thought that gave me the solution? If I keep following causes backward, the clean boundary around "my achievement" becomes difficult to find.

So when something goes well, I want to spend 20 seconds actually tracing it — not as forced humility, but as investigation. Then instead of pride in possession, I may get something closer to gratitude + responsibility: this came through me, among many other causes. Now what should I do with it?

Yasser Zarhloul
