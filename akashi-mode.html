Akashi Mode

A Mode Of Being

Some states aren't moods and aren't skills. They're closer to a stance: a particular way of standing in relation to a situation that changes what the situation is made of. You don't get into one by trying harder. You get into one by adopting a different account of what's happening.

Akashi Mode is the name I use for one of these. The shape of it is roughly: complete legibility of the board, no wasted motion, and an almost unreasonable calm about outcomes — not because they don't matter, but because the work of deciding was done further upstream than anyone watching can see.

What it isn't

It isn't confidence, which is a claim about yourself. It isn't dominance, which is a claim about others. Both are loud and both are reactive — they need an audience to stay stable. This is quieter and more boring than either: you've simply already looked, and there's nothing left to be startled by.

The conditions for it

It seems to need three things at once, and the reason it's rare is that they normally trade off against each other:

  • Resolution. Seeing the situation at a fine enough grain that the moving parts are individually visible, not blurred into a mood.
  • Compression. Holding all of that in a frame small enough to act from. Resolution without compression is just anxiety.
  • Detachment from the result. Not indifference — the specific freedom of having priced the downside honestly, in advance.

How it fails

The failure mode is that the stance becomes a performance of itself. The moment the calm needs to be witnessed, it's gone — you're managing an impression rather than reading a board. I don't think there's a reliable way to prevent this. There's only noticing it, which is usually late.
